# Viral Writing Playbook v1.2

How we write hooks, slides, clips and captions for every SideStep account (mother characters and feeders). Built Oct 16, 2026 from a full read of Brendan Kane's "The Guide to Going Viral" (all three parts), rewritten in our own words and mapped to our system. Kane's examples are summarized for learning; none of his lines or anyone else's go into our posts.

v1.1 and v1.2 (Oct 16, Eric's review): v1.0 told writers to "never state the problem," and the launch hooks came out as clever punchlines nobody could decode without the slides, about a 2 out of 10 on pain. The standard below replaces that rule and wins over anything later in this doc.

Where this sits:
- `claude/sidestep-social-strategy.md` says WHAT to talk about (tensions T1 to T13, audiences A1 to A6, pillars P1 to P4, accounts F1 to F9).
- `claude/sidestep-script-kit.md` says what we may CLAIM (the claims sheet wins over anything here).
- This playbook says HOW to write it so it gets watched, swiped and followed.

## THE STANDARD (read this first, v1.2, Oct 16)

This section wins over everything later in the playbook. It comes from Eric's line-by-line review of the launch posts.

**Why we post.** Most posts are not ads. They show people that someone else feels their pain, and they name it so exactly that people share it with others who feel it too. The reaction we want on slide 1: "HOLY SHIT, THEY KNOW ME." Shares and follows come from that, not from clever.

**Where:** slideshows are TikTok only. Instagram gets short reels later (parked). Write for TikTok.

**The hook (slide 1)**
- **Names the job situation, every time.** A job application, a job interview, a job offer, a recruiter, a salary, a resume. "Applied," "round 5" or "took $9,000 less" alone could be anything. If a stranger can't tell it's about the job hunt, they swipe.
- **Says exactly what happened:** the number, the thing, the moment. Never a pointer like "how bad it is" or "it did not go how i thought."
- **Hurts.** 7 or higher out of 10: money, rent, years lost, shame, family asking, feeling like it's you. Mild annoyance is a 2.
- **Reads in one second, with no image.** About 15 words or fewer. No labels or stage directions ("Exhibit 3:", "Levels of...", "Calendar invite, 4:55pm"), no stacked quotes.
- **Opens a loop** the slides close. The punchline never sits on slide 1.

**The voice: your friend in the group chat, fr fr.** This is TikTok, not a court of law. We are not marketing; we are the peer group, frustrated together.
- **Type like people text:** "man 54 f'ing boxes to fill on that job app fam?????????? 54!!!!!! that wuz AFTER i gave the resume" (Eric, the reference line).
- Open with a feeling or sound: man, uggh, yo, bro, ok so, not me.
- Lowercase, fragments, run-ons. CAPS for the shock word. Stacked ?????? and !!!!!! when it's outrageous. Repeat the number in disbelief ("FOUR.").
- Texting spellings: wuz, ur, u, rn, ngl, fr, bc, ppl, yrs, $.
- Censored swearing is fine: f'ing.
- Slang where it lands naturally, one or two per post: no cap, NPC, mid, an L / a W, bet, salty, lowkey, bestie, fam, make it make sense, we're cooked, i'm crying, hits different, that's crazy work.
- Self-roast, system rage. The joke is on me; the anger is at the process. Never at the viewer.
- People talk like people: the roommate goes "bro... this resume isn't for any job."
- Boomer test: if it could run on LinkedIn or in a print ad, rewrite it.
- Older job seekers (later, for Instagram and Facebook reels): the same pain in Facebook-comment voice ("Is it just me or...", full sentences, "Anyone else??"). Not for TikTok slideshows.

**The story (slides 2 to 6)**
- One thread: every slide stays on the hook's story. No side jokes from other pains.
- Slide 2: the setup that raises the stakes. Slide 3 or 4: the turn, often a person reacting (mom, roommate, recruiter, a friend in HR). Slide 5: the payoff (the truth, the twist, the absurd detail; this is where the clever jokes and moments go). Slide 6: relief or recognition ("it's not you") or a question the comments answer.
- Every slide makes sense to a stranger with no backstory (the clarity test).
- 4 to 14 words a slide, same voice as the hook.
- Every slide gets its own image line that shows that beat. The picture carries half the story. Feeder accounts show hands, screens, rooms and objects, never faces.

**The feeling test (every post).** It must hit at least one hard: super painful, super frustrating, shows how bad work and job hunting are right now, or super annoying. If all four are "kind of," it doesn't run.

**The stop test (every hook, before anything else).** Any "no" means rewrite.
1. Would a stranger scrolling past stop on this line alone?
2. Is it clearly about a job, on one read, with no image?
3. Does it say exactly what happened?
4. Is the pain 7 or higher?
5. Does it sound like a text from a friend, not a brand?

**SideStep endings (the 20% product posts)**
- Most posts never mention SideStep. On product posts, slide 6 is a friend's tip that fixes the exact pain from slide 1: "my friend told me about SideStep fr fr..."
- Only use it when SideStep really fixes that pain. The honest endings:
  - retyping the resume into boxes: "one click fills the whole app. i just check it and hit submit"
  - hours on jobs you don't fit: "it shows how u match before u apply"
  - rewriting the resume for every job: "it shows exactly what to add from stuff i actually did"
  - paying monthly for job tools: "$99 once. no subscription"
- Never: "stop getting ghosted," "stop getting passed over," "it applies for you." SideStep can't promise replies, and it never submits for you.
- Ghosting, fake jobs, layoffs, salary games, scams and interview rounds stay pure pain. No SideStep on those.
- "Ad:" opens the caption. Claims sheet only.

**Examples that pass**
- man 54 f'ing boxes to fill on that job app fam?????????? 54!!!!!! that wuz AFTER i gave the resume
- a guy at my bar is on round 6 for a job that pays less than my tips
- this job's been "actively hiring" since JANUARY. i applied 3 times. hiring WHO bro
- 4 rounds of job interviews and they ghosted me. FOUR. not even a "no thx" ngl i'm tight
- took a job offer for 9k less than i wanted bc i had like 5 weeks of $ left. i hate it here

**Examples that fail, and why**
- "set a 6am alarm to apply in the first hour. 100+ people already beat me" (apply for what? no job word)
- "5 months since i graduated and i still haven't told my family how bad it is" (how bad what? says nothing)
- "I've applied to 300 jobs since graduation and I'm starting to think it's me." (clear, but copywriter voice)
- "Levels of ghosted after a final round. Level 5 asks for feedback." (a label plus a punchline)
- "My diploma arrived in May. It's still in the tube. It's scared." (a joke you have to decode)
- "the 'casual' final round at a bar is NOT casual" (the setup is never explained)

**Where the rest of this playbook fits now:** moments, structures, lenses and Kane's techniques shape slides 2 to 6 and the payoff. They never replace a plain, painful slide 1.

## 0. The one-page version

1. **Say the pain plainly on slide 1; show it happening on slides 2 to 6.** "Ghosting is bad" is a topic with no pain and no loop. "They ghosted me after 5 interviews. Then they reposted the same job." is a hook. The moment ("Friday number 6") is the story behind it. (v1.1: replaces "never state the problem.")
2. **Pick the effect first.** Before a word is written, name what the viewer should feel in the first second: "that's literally me," "wait, what?", "I need to know how this ends." No named effect, no post.
3. **Pour the moment into a proven structure.** A format (Levels, Two Characters, Untold Story, Teaser, Jenga) carries the story; the topic doesn't.
4. **Run it through a lens.** A frame from somewhere else (nature documentary, Yelp review, delivery tracker, invoice) makes the familiar feel new. Kane: clever = smart + unexpected.
5. **Every slide leaves a question.** Setup and punchline never share a slide. The reveal waits.
6. **Feelings, Facts, Fun.** Each post carries at least two. No "should," "need to," "must." CTAs are questions or shrugs, never orders.
7. **Write 20, ship 1.** Rank every hook 1 (best) to 5. Nothing worse than a 2 ships.
8. **Check it before it posts** against the 10-point benchmark (section 9).

The brief formula that replaces "tension + angle":

> **PAIN HOOK (plain, 7+ out of 10, opens a loop) + 6-SLIDE STORY (moment, structure or lens, turn on 3 or 4, payoff on 5) + EFFECT + production format.** The tension code (T1 to T13) is a tag on the row.

## 1. Why our copy reads generic (the diagnosis)

Kane's sharpest finding for us comes from comparing hit and flop videos by the same creator: the flops give the answer away in the title. "The Real Reason Ice Is Slippery" tells you the topic and the shape of the answer, so there's nothing to find out. The hits imply a hidden story ("Your City Is Full of Fake Buildings").

Our weak hooks do the same thing four ways:
- **Topic label:** "Let's talk about ghost jobs." The viewer already knows the topic exists.
- **Stat first:** "About 254 people apply to every job." True and sourced, but it's the conclusion, delivered before any reason to care.
- **Restated pain:** "Interviews take too many rounds." The viewer lives this; hearing it back gives them nothing new.
- **First idea shipped:** we wrote one angle per slot and posted it. Kane's teams write about 100 ideas for every one they make.

The fix is not better adjectives. It's a different starting point: start from a scene, a frame and an effect, and let the problem show itself.

## 2. Effect on the viewer (EOV): decide this first

Kane separates the **tactic** (what's on screen: text box, music, a prop) from the **effect** (what the viewer feels). Copying tactics from a hit fails because the effect is what worked. So every brief starts with the effect.

Our effect menu (pick one for slide 1, one for the payoff):

| Code | Effect | Typical trigger |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E1 | "That's literally me" | A detail only a job seeker would know (page 4 of a portal, the 2:14am timestamp) |
| E2 | "Wait, what?" | A hidden layer under something familiar |
| E3 | "I need to know how this ends" | An end state shown first, or a countdown |
| E4 | "LMAO" | The familiar pushed one notch too far |
| E5 | "Sending this to someone" | A line that says what the viewer couldn't, about a person they know |
| E6 | "So it's not me" | A fact that moves blame from the viewer to the system |
| E7 | "I never saw it that way" | A belief flipped (perspective shift) |
| E8 | "Screenshotting this" | One usable thing, doable tonight |

A good post moves through two or three: E1 on slide 1, E3 through the middle, E4 or E7 on the payoff, E5 in the caption. Our content roof ("The job market is broken. You're not.") is E6, delivered by showing, never by saying.

## 3. Moments: the problem in flight

A **moment** is one specific scene of the problem happening, small enough to fit on a slide or in a 6-second clip. It is never a conclusion.

Moment card (the format for the moment bank):
- **When:** day and time, with a timestamp if it helps ("Sunday, 9:40pm").
- **Where:** the real place (couch, car in a parking lot, bathroom at a job fair, kitchen at 6am).
- **What's on screen:** the exact thing being looked at (page 4 of 6, the status bar, the email preview line).
- **The tell:** one detail only a real job seeker knows ("Start date (MM/YYYY)," the "Dear Candidate" opener, a CAPTCHA after 40 minutes of typing).
- **Tag:** tension code and audience code.

Six seed moments (the full bank of 100+ is the next build):
1. Sunday 9:40pm, laptop on a couch pillow. Page 4 of 6 of a portal asks for the work history uploaded on page 1. The cursor blinks in "Start date (MM/YYYY)." T13, A5.
2. 11:52pm submit. 2:14am email preview on a lock screen: "Thank you for your interest in..." T3, A5.
3. A sticky note on a laptop lid with tally marks, one per Friday since "we'll talk Friday." T5, A1.
4. A posting tab open for months. "Actively hiring" badge. Same typo in the title on every repost. T2, A5.
5. A parent at the kitchen table: "Did you get it?" The answer: "Which round?" T6, A1.
6. A checkout page with fine print that renews weekly, read with a magnifying-glass emoji energy. T9, A2 (product-adjacent, category only).

Rules for moments:
- Real light, real places (matches the image rules). The moment should work as a still.
- Absurd specificity beats adjectives. "11 minutes late" beats "very late."
- Story details (rounds, timestamps, tally marks) can be invented for the bit. **Market numbers can't.** Any number that reads like a stat comes from the claims sheet or the strategy doc, with a source.
- Characters never get a SideStep result inside a moment ("got the interview because...").

## 4. Structures (Kane's formats, mapped to us)

A structure is the story's skeleton. It lasts for years; trends last weeks. Kane's advice is to get world class at one or two per account rather than rotating. Each feeder owns one primary and one backup; each mother account owns one signature structure.

### S1. Levels
Show the same thing at rising levels (bad to good, mild to unhinged). The promise of a climb holds people to the top level.
- Slideshow: slide 1 names the ladder; slides 2 to 5 climb; slide 6 is the top level or the twist.
- Best for: T13 labor tax, T6 rounds, T5 ghosting. Natural product home: the top level can be the tailored, matched approach (P2, P3).
- Hook: "Levels of getting ghosted. Level 5 involves a LinkedIn 'congrats on the new role' for the person they hired."

### S2. Two Characters, One Lightbulb
A novice states a common belief with confidence; an insider corrects it; one lightbulb moment; one usable takeaway. Kane's best place for a soft product, because the expert's fix can be the category SideStep sits in.
- Slideshow: a text thread or two-column meme. Clip: one character, two roles (outfit or hair change), or two roster characters.
- One aha per post. No side jokes that stall it. Max 4 lines each.
- Best for: T3, T13, T1. Product posts P2 and P3.
- Hook: "Me: sent 200 applications, the market is dead. My friend in HR: 'how many different resumes was that?'"

### S3. Untold Story (anchor plus hidden layer)
Start on something everyone knows, promise what's underneath, peel it a layer per slide, confirm the big reveal only at the end.
- Rules: anchor on the familiar object (the "actively hiring" badge, the "we'll keep your resume on file" email), never assume they saw part 1, one focal point per slide.
- Best for: T2 ghost jobs, T3 robot rejection, T4 AI vs AI, F1, F4.
- Hook: "This posting has said 'actively hiring' since before my lease started. I went looking for who it's hiring."

### S4. Is It Worth It?
Imply the viewer may be overpaying or being fooled, tear the thing apart, verdict at the end.
- Best for: T6 unpaid work (invoice the take-home), T9 money clock, and the competitor category (P4).
- Category only in short-form: "the monthly kind," never a named competitor (strategy section 10).
- Hook: "I did a 'quick' take-home for a job I didn't get. Was it worth it? Let's invoice it."

### S5. Testing Viral Hacks
A character tries popular job advice under clear rules and keeps a scorecard. Kane's woodworking case: the rules and the trial make the viewer a participant.
- Report process and absurdity, never a SideStep result. Hacks that "trick" the screener get tested and debunked, never endorsed.
- Best for: mother accounts (Callie's signature fit), T1, T3.
- Hook: "Testing viral job hacks so my bank account doesn't have to. Hack 1: 'just walk in with a paper resume.'"

### S6. Teaser open
Open on the end state at its most dramatic, cut before the outcome, rewind to the start, pay off at the end.
- Best for: T5, T6, clips with a "caught you" ending.
- Hook: "Day 41. The email finally came. Subject line: 'Quick question!' Let me back up."

### S7. Jenga carousel (the question chain)
One big question on slide 1. Each slide answers a small question and opens the next. The real answer lands last.
- Every slide ends on an open loop; that's what drives the swipe.
- Best for: T3, T1, stat slideshows (it's how a stat stops being a lecture).
- Hook: "Why did 200 applications get zero calls? It's not the reason anyone told me."

### S8. Visual Metaphor
An everyday object acts out the problem, gets worse on screen, then the reframe. The object must be recognizable in frame 1 and must carry potential energy (something about to happen).
- Slideshow: a shredder labeled "the filter," a stack of 254 resumes shrinking slide by slide. Clip: a calm action with a prop (a tally mark, a progress bar).
- Best for: T3, T10, T13.
- Hook: "This is my resume. This is the filter. Watch."

### S9. Man on the Street (as text bubbles)
Ask several people one question, save the best answer for last. We fake it honestly: text bubbles or a character asking her roster friends, never real strangers' faces.
- Best for: T6, T1, prompt posts that feed the comment engine.
- Hook: "Asked 5 people their worst interview round. Number 4 is still in it."

### S10. Challenge series
A character runs a set-length challenge, organized by obstacles, not by day count. Stakes stated up front. Duration matched to difficulty (14 days, not 100).
- Show the process. No outcome claims tied to SideStep.
- Best for: mother accounts, T13, T1.
- Hook: "14 days. One rule: no applying to a job I don't match. Day 1 already hurts."

### S11. Contrast pair
Two things side by side: what they say vs what they mean, the post vs the job, the same week for two people. Kane rates contrast as one of the most dependable drivers.
- Best for: F4 Recruiter Translator, T7, T12.
- Hook: "Job post: 'fast-paced environment.' Translation: three people quit and you're all of them."

### S12. Countdown (potential energy)
A clock, a meter, a rent date or a draining bar that the viewer watches run.
- Best for: T9, T10, F7.
- Hook: "Rent is due in 9 days. The recruiter said 'end of week' three weeks ago."

## 5. Lenses (the frame borrowed from somewhere else)

A lens makes a familiar moment feel new by showing it through an unrelated, everyday frame. This is Kane's "cleverness" (smart + unexpected) and his "bridge to a popular topic." Structures S1 to S12 above double as lenses; these thirteen are pure frames.

| # | Lens | What it does | Best for | Sample hook |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| L13 | Nature documentary | Narrates hiring like wildlife | T5, T6 | "Here we observe the recruiter in its natural habitat. It has seen the message. It will not respond." |
| L14 | Museum exhibit | Puts a rejection on a placard | T3, T5 (F2 owns) | "Exhibit 4: a rejection that arrived before the confirmation email." |
| L15 | Review and rating | Rates rejections, portals, rounds like restaurants | T3, T13 | "Rating every portal I filled out this week. One star: the one with a CAPTCHA on page 6." |
| L16 | Dating parallel | The job hunt as a situationship | T5, T1 | "Recruiters and situationships. A side-by-side. They both said 'let's talk soon.'" |
| L17 | Delivery tracker | Application status as a stuck package | T1 | "My application has said 'in review' longer than a package stuck in customs." |
| L18 | Fine print | Reads the terms like a contract | T6, T9, T11 | "Reading the fine print on a 'quick' take-home with me." |
| L19 | Breakup text | The job writes to you | T5 | "The job I didn't get just texted me." |
| L20 | Personification | The resume, degree or posting has feelings | T7, T3 | "My degree finally got a job. Not in its field." |
| L21 | Invoice and timesheet | Puts a price on unpaid work | T6, T13 | "Billing the company for round 4. Line item 1: the deck." |
| L22 | "Previously on" recap | A season recap of one job hunt | T6 | "Previously on Round 7: two CEOs, one reorg, zero offers." |
| L23 | Subtitles | Captions what's really being said | T5, F4 | "Turning on subtitles for my recruiter call." |
| L24 | Forecast and horoscope | Predicts the week like weather | T8, T10 | "Job market forecast: 80% chance of 'we'll keep your resume on file.'" |
| L25 | Group chat | The reaction of a friend, mom or roommate in a thread | Any (our strongest default) | "My mom: 'just call them.' Me: 'there is no number. there is a chatbot named Ava.'" |

Lens rules:
- The lens is a frame, not a person or brand. No real people, no shows or songs by name in images, no logos. "A nature documentary voice" is fine; a named narrator isn't.
- Kane says name the thing the masses already know. Our guardrail says don't mock real companies by name. The resolution: name the universal object or experience (the portal that makes you retype your resume, the one-click apply button, the "actively hiring" badge), not the brand.
- A lens should make the moment clearer, not hide it. If a viewer needs the caption to get the joke, swap the lens.

## 6. Writing techniques

### 6.1 Every slide leaves a question (Jenga)
Kane borrows Jenga: everyone knows the tower falls; each pulled block is the suspense. Each slide resolves one small question and opens the next. Test: cover the next slide. If the viewer has no reason to want it, rewrite this one.

### 6.2 Complete the scale
Kane's music analogy: play do-re-mi up to the last note and stop, and people feel unsettled. A hook is a promise; the last slide must pay it, clearly and satisfyingly. Never end a slideshow on the problem with nothing resolved (a laugh counts as a resolution).

### 6.3 One more layer
A single-layer post (rating job posts) is flat. Add a second layer (rated by the ex-recruiter) and a third (she applies to the worst one and reports next post). Two or three layers is the sweet spot.

### 6.4 One notch too far (absurdity)
A familiar moment plus one twist. Memory research Kane cites: the familiar makes it recognizable, the twist makes it stick. Push the real situation one step past real, not ten. "Round 6: a take-home" is real. "Round 6: a take-home to redesign their app, due by their all-hands" is one notch.

### 6.5 The "You know how...?" chain
Line up statements the viewer nods at, then turn. Great for clips and multi-slide setups.
> "You know how every application asks for your resume? And then has you type the whole thing into boxes? And then asks if you're human?"
If the nods don't come, the analogy is wrong.

### 6.6 Hook Point math (compound the pain)
Turn a small, known pain into a big, vivid number. Kane's example compounds 15 hours a week into about 30 days a year. Ours, inside the claims sheet:
- "Long applications take 20 to 30 minutes by hand. Ten a week is a part-time job that pays in 'Thank you for your interest.'"
- "The average search runs about 26 weeks. That's a lot of Sundays in 'Start date (MM/YYYY).'"
Only pain-size numbers and sourced market stats. Never a time-saved number other than the site's 90%.

### 6.7 Skeleton stealing (template extraction)
Take a title that already works anywhere, strip the topic, keep the skeleton, pour ours in. Structure only, never anyone's words or jokes.
- "$1 vs $1,000,000 [thing]" becomes "The $0 resume vs the 'career coach' resume."
- "[thing] hacks you'll actually use" becomes "3 job search moves you'll actually use (none are 'network more')."
- "Testing viral [thing]" becomes S5.
- "Levels of [thing]" becomes S1.
- "Things [person] won't say out loud" becomes the ex-recruiter's series.

### 6.8 Potential energy in frame 1
The first image should look like something is about to happen: a cursor over "Submit," a phone face down buzzing, a match struck. Recognizable at a glance, or no expectation forms.

### 6.9 Teaser cut
Show a late moment, cut right before the outcome, rewind. Works in slideshows ("Slide 1: Day 41. Slide 2: How it started.") and clips.

### 6.10 Withhold the reveal
Kane's street photographer got requests to show the final photo sooner; when he did, views dropped. Commenters asking "just tell us the tool" are not the brief. The product, the answer and the twist stay late.

### 6.11 Linear, mute-readable, one focal point
About 60% of feed viewers watch muted (Kane's figure). One message at a time: either the character's expression or the caption carries the joke, not both competing. One idea per slide, large text, time to read it.

## 7. The Communication Algorithm (how it's said)

Kane's Part III adapts a psychology model with six styles. Each person leans on one:

| Style | Share | Responds to | Our use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Feelings | 30% | Being understood, warmth | The validation line: "I feel like I'm applying into a void with an autoresponder." |
| Facts | 25% | Data, specifics, sources | One sourced number, framed as a reveal, not a lecture |
| Fun | 20% | Reactions, humor, energy | Our native register: snark, slang, absurd specifics |
| Opinions | 10% | Values, beliefs | Mostly avoid |
| Imagination | 10% | Reflection, visualizing | Rarely |
| Actions | 5% | Directives, bottom lines | Avoid in copy (an exec's natural voice, which is the trap) |

**The Five Rules** (write for the big three, avoid the two that repel):
1. **Feelings:** include one line that validates before it jokes. Roast the system, never the viewer. No "it's not a big deal."
2. **Facts:** one concrete, sourced number or specific per post where it fits. Don't start a sentence with "You." Move it to the middle or end ("The resume sent to 200 jobs was the wrong resume. Maybe yours too?").
3. **Fun:** reactions, slang, energy, a prop, a cut. Never a static, monotone talking head.
4. **Non-values:** cut "should," "ought," "need to," "must," "we believe," and soapboxing about what companies ought to do. Translate each into a feeling, a fact or a joke.
5. **Non-autocratic:** no orders. Turn commands into questions (democratic), warm offers (benevolent) or shrugs (laid-back).

Rewrites:
- "You should tailor every resume." becomes "Does yours sound like you when you read it out loud?" (democratic) or "The tailored one got read. The other 199 got filtered." (fact-style story beat, no outcome claim for SideStep).
- "Companies need to stop ghosting." becomes "53% of us got ghosted this year (iHire, 2026). The other 47% are lying."
- "Download SideStep now." becomes "Want to see it fill a form? It's in bio." / "Left the link in bio for whoever's up at 2am applying." / "Link's up there if you're nosy."

**The listing stack** for product captions (Kane's house-listing example, all styles in a few lines): a fact (what it does, from the claims sheet), a warm line (who it's for), a feeling (the relief), a joke, and a question CTA. Example:
> Ad: SideStep shows how you match before you apply and fills the form in one click. You review and hit submit. Made for the 2am applicants. $99 once, no subscription. Want to see the before and after? Link in bio.

**Triple F pass:** before any script ships, find one Feeling line, one Fact, one Fun beat. Then scan for "you should," "need to," "must," "just," "buy," "download now."

Note on "don't start with You": a "POV:" opener is fine; it's a frame, not an instruction.

## 8. Ideation: write 20, ship 1

Kane's ratio is about 100 ideas for every one made. At our volume, Claude does the volume and Eric sees the shortlist.

**Per account, per week:**
1. Pull 20 moments from the bank for the account's tensions.
2. For each, write the hook through 2 different lenses or structures. That's 40 candidates.
3. Rank every one 1 to 5 (below). Kill everything rated 3 to 5.
4. Workshop the top 1s: one more layer, a sharper tell, a better effect.
5. Fill the week's slots from the ranked backlog. Slots are fixed; ideas aren't (Kane's "triangle, not set plays": choose each day from the best available given news and results).
6. Audit the shortlist for over-concentration: if every hook is a roast, force in a lens, a perspective flip and a Levels.

**The 1 to 5 ladder** (v1.1: scores stopping power and pain first, cleverness last):
- **5:** a topic, a label or a news-style headline. "Ghosting is bad." "Levels of ghosted."
- **4:** a punchline or joke that needs the slides or an image to make sense. "My diploma is still in the tube. It's scared."
- **3:** plain and clear, but mild pain or no open loop. "Got ghosted after a final round."
- **2:** plain, painful (7+), with an open loop. "They ghosted me after 5 interviews and I still don't know why."
- **1:** all of 2, plus a sharp specific or twist that makes it shareable. "They ghosted me after 5 interviews. Then they reposted the same job."

**The kill test** (any "no" kills it):
1. The stop test (THE STANDARD above): would a stranger stop, is it clear on one read with no image, is the pain 7+?
2. What does the viewer get at the end (relief, recognition, a laugh, a usable thing)?
3. Where is the turn?

## 9. The pre-post benchmark (10 points)

Kane checks every video side by side with a proven hit in the same format before posting. Our version, for every slideshow and clip:

Required (all four):
1. **First second:** passes the stop test: plain words, the pain is clear on one read with no image, 7+ on pain, and a loop only swiping closes.
2. **Effect named:** the brief lists the slide 1 effect and the payoff effect, and the post delivers them.
3. **Tension:** a question is open on every slide until the payoff, and the payoff completes the scale.
4. **Viewer connection:** singular "you," head-voice language, at least one validating line, mute-readable.

At least two of:
5. **Generalist:** someone not job hunting today still gets it (everyone's been ghosted, everyone hates forms).
6. **Perspective shift:** a belief flips.
7. **Cleverness:** two unrelated things joined in a way that makes sense.
8. **Absurdity:** one notch too far.

Hygiene (all):
9. **Language:** no should, need, must, just-before-an-instruction, hustle words; CTA is a question or shrug; Feelings, Facts, Fun at least two present.
10. **Rules:** claims sheet only, every stat sourced, "Ad:" first on product captions, no real people or logos, 5 hashtags max, no em or en dashes.

Ship at 4 of 4 required, 2 of 4 drivers, and full hygiene. When a post flops, run the same 10 points on it against a winner in the same format: if the execution falls short, fix execution; if it matches and still flops, the format or moment is the problem.

## 10. Research: find our Gold before we guess

Kane's method: pick one format, find 5 to 10 creators who use it consistently, sort their posts into **Gold** (breakouts), **Silver** (their usual, the most common view count) and **Bronze** (below their median), and keep only what shows up in Gold and not in Bronze. A trait in both isn't the reason.

How we run it without a research team:
1. For each of our 3 most-used structures (S3, S11, S6 to start), Eric saves 5 Gold and 5 Bronze posts from job, career or corporate-humor accounts (any niche works if the structure matches), with view counts.
2. Screenshots of slide 1 and the payoff slide come into a chat; Claude tiers them and writes the 3 to 5 drivers per format.
3. Those drivers become the format's extra benchmark lines and its Gold reference for side-by-side checks.
4. Once each feeder has 30+ posts, run the same sort on our own accounts. Our best post becomes the new reference.

Filters Kane uses: consistent creators only (not one lucky hit), no celebrity or brand accounts, and a likes-to-views ratio under 1% usually means paid reach, so skip it.

## 11. Reading results (diagnose with Kane, decide with ours)

- **Decide** with our metrics: follows per post first, then saves and shares, then profile visits, then signups by tracked link. Our decision tiers stand (50K+ views: make 3 variations; under 1K twice: drop the hook family).
- **Diagnose** with Kane's ratios, where the platform shows them:
  - Low early hold (3-second view rate, first-slide drop) means a hook problem. Rewrite slide 1 or the first second only.
  - Good hold, low completion or swipe-through means a middle problem: find the slide where people leave and give it an open loop.
  - Good completion, low follows means a payoff or identity problem: the account's signature isn't clear yet.
- Compare accounts by ratios, never raw views; reach is uneven across feeders.
- When a post pops, log why (structure, lens, effect, tell) on the row, so the factory can make the 3 variations on purpose.

## 12. Product posts the Kane way (the 20%)

Kane's branding test: prove a format works unbranded, then add branding one way at a time and measure the drop (his example: a logo cost a format 75% of its views). Our 20% stays, but each product post is now a measured test.

Product placements that fit Kane's structures, all inside the claims sheet:
- **S2 Two Characters:** the insider's fix is "see how you match before you apply." SideStep is named once, late.
- **S1 Levels:** the top level is the matched, tailored application. "Surgical, not a rewrite."
- **Satisfying before and after (mock UI):** an empty 40-field form, then filled in one click, "you review and hit submit."
- **S4 Is It Worth It? (category only):** subscriptions that bill until you cancel vs "$99 once, a full year, no auto-renew."
- **S10 Challenge:** SideStep visible in the workflow, never credited with a result.

Placement tests to rotate one at a time across feeders, and log: caption only, slide 6 only, slide 5 with the mock UI, mentioned by the mother account. Watch follows per post against the same account's non-product posts.

Never on any product post: a character's personal hiring result, "beats the ATS," auto-apply, ghost-job detection, phone, local-first, any outcome claim beyond the site's approved hero lines.

## 13. Where Kane and our system disagree (and what we do)

| Kane says | Our system | What we do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Post less; master one format before adding volume | 2 to 3 a day across 20+ accounts | Volume is the test engine: each account tests one variable, so the network learns at Kane's pace across many lanes. The quality floor is the ranking: nothing worse than a 2 ships. Mother accounts lean to fewer, stronger posts. |
| Calendars and fixed schedules are a waste | Two-week launch calendar | The calendar holds slots, not ideas. Slots fill weekly from the ranked backlog. |
| Fun means big movement and animated faces | Calm action rule for AI video (big facial acting distorts faces) | Fun comes from props, secondary motion, edits, on-screen text and sound. The face stays calm. Real dancing uses the recreate-from-a-real-clip recipe. |
| Prove unbranded first, then add branding | 20% product from day 1 | Product never in the hook (already a rule); each product post is a placement test with its drop measured. |
| Name the thing people know | No mocking real companies by name | Name the universal experience or object, not the brand. |
| Hashtags barely matter | 3 to 5 per post | Keep them; they're cheap. Spend effort on slide 1 and the caption's first line. |
| Don't listen to your audience | Comment engine mines comments | Comments are an input for moments and language, never the brief. Reveals stay late even when commenters ask. |
| Research Gold before making anything | Launched without a swipe file | Run section 10 during weeks 1 and 2; drivers feed week 3. |

## 15. Studio implications (parking-lot candidates, not current builds)

- **Brief fields:** effect (E1 to E8), moment ID, lens or structure ID, rank (1 to 5), Gold reference link. These join the tension, audience, pillar and product tags on every row.
- **Idea backlog with ranking:** 40 candidates per account per week, ranked, top ones fill slots.
- **Benchmark drawer:** the 10-point check beside each draft, with the format's Gold reference side by side.
- **Banned-word lint:** flags should, need to, must, just, hustle words, "You" at sentence start, and dashes.
- **Results log:** why a post won (structure, lens, effect, tell), so variations are made on purpose.

## 16. Kane's numbers worth keeping

- About 15 posts are shown from a pool of about 150,000 when someone opens an app. We compete with everything, not with job content.
- About 10% of Instagram followers see a given post.
- About 60% of feed viewers watch muted.
- Likes-to-views under 1% usually signals paid reach (skip as a research reference).
- Communication styles: Feelings 30%, Facts 25%, Fun 20%, Opinions 10%, Imagination 10%, Actions 5%. Writing for the first three while avoiding values and orders reaches up to about 85%.
- GSB research: at least 5 Gold, 5 Silver, 5 Bronze per creator; 3 to 5 drivers per format; content from the last 1 to 2 years.
- Ideas: about 100 per one made; the ideation sheet starts at 20 with a reference for each.
